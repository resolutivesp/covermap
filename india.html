CoverMap India — where the standard antivenom fails the local snakes

India carries the world's largest snakebite burden (~58,000 deaths/yr, Million Death Study). Unlike West Africa, the lever is not "which product" — it is that the single southern-sourced polyvalent (ASV) underperforms regionally and misses ~20 non-Big-Four species. This maps where that gap is deadliest.

Mortality is anchored to published national data. Modelled mortality is 55,656/yr — within 4.0% of the Million Death Study point estimate (~58,000), using MDS state death rates transcribed verbatim from the published table (Table 3, 2010–2014) — including its own catch-all rows for the Northeastern states (0.7/100,000) and all other states (3.2/100,000), so no assumed state rate remains. Independent check: the model's implied national rate is 4.6/100,000 against Table 3's published All-India row of 4.5/100,000, and inside the GBD 2019 uncertainty interval (29,600–64,100). Mortality is distributed within each state 94% rural / 6% urban, because the MDS itself finds ~94% of India's snakebite deaths occur in rural areas — this leaves every state total unchanged but stops metros from dominating the priority list.
The coverage-gap percentage is a model output, not a measurement — read it as an order of magnitude. No published figure exists for the share of Indian snakebite burden the standard ASV fails to cover, at national or state level. We searched for one specifically; the review paper whose entire thesis is "look beyond the Big Four" (Menon 2025) does not state one, and the national action plan proposes Regional Venom Centres precisely because the data does not exist. Our adequacy values are therefore evidence-informed ordinal tiers (45% NW · 35% NE · 30% Andamans · 65% rest of mainland · 75% southern sourcing zone), not measured fractions. An adversarial review of the antivenomics literature confirmed the direction of every tier and supported no precise level; the error sign of the 36.8% is unknown. We report it because the ranking is decision-useful, and we refuse to imply precision it does not have.
What IS published and hard — the anchors that carry this case:
  • 68.4% — clinical ASV non-response in E. c. sochureki envenoming, NW India (63/92 patients; median 22 vials; all 9 deaths were non-responders) (Gopalakrishnan 2025, Trans R Soc Trop Med Hyg 119(8):943)
  • 66.19% — share of identified venomous bites caused by green pit viper / Salazar's pit viper (NO ASV label coverage), Demow CHC, Assam (Menon 2025, Trans R Soc Trop Med Hyg 119(9):1016)
  • ~0.25 — Bharat ASV potency vs N. sagittifera relative to its 0.6 mg/ml marketed claim, Andaman & Nicobar (Premium Serums: completely ineffective) (Attarde 2021, Front Pharmacol 12:768210)
  • 32.6% — Hypnale hypnale share of species-identified snakebites, Kerala (a non-Big-Four species with no ASV coverage) (Menon 2025 citing a Kerala tertiary series)
These are attributable, sub-national and independent of our model. They are the strongest evidence that the gap is real; the map's job is to say where it concentrates, not to restate them.

The regional pattern: a southern-sourced antivenom can't cover pan-India venom

Indian ASV is raised on southern (Tamil Nadu/Irula) venoms. Published antivenomics shows: against the Rajasthan desert cobra the antivenom was completely ineffective — the highest dose tested failed to protect (Senji Laxme 2021); against Punjab Russell's viper it neutralised at 0.39 mg/ml versus 0.84–0.99 mg/ml for every other Indian population tested, against a marketed claim of 0.60 (Senji Laxme 2021); and it has no label coverage at all for green pit vipers, Hypnale, sea snakes or Bungarus niger. Darker = larger uncovered share.

Correction: an earlier draft compressed the Punjab result to "−35%", which is ambiguous — that is the shortfall against the marketed claim, whereas the shortfall against other Indian populations is 56–61%. The figures above are stated in full instead.

The decision it changes — quantified

India has no product-placement optimiser: access is not the binding gap here (under 1% of burden sits more than 50 km from a hospital). The decision this tool informs is different. Region-specific antivenom, and the Regional Venom Centres India's national action plan proposes, have to be rolled out somewhere first. This is what each choice reaches.

Each row reports the share of the coverage-gap burden that sits in the districts targeted — a reach measure, exactly parallel to "within reach" in the West-African demonstrators. It is not a claim that deploying there averts those deaths, and it inherits the ordinal uncertainty of the adequacy tiers above.

Two things this table says — one encouraging, one not.

Targeting beats geography. The tool's top 50 districts reach 23.8% of the coverage-gap burden; targeting all 133 districts of the northwest reaches 22.1%. Roughly the same reach for 83 fewer districts, because the ranking crosses state lines to follow burden × gap rather than administrative boundaries. For a programme deciding where to site the first Regional Venom Centres, that is the practical output.

But India has no small-set solution, and we will not pretend otherwise. The top 100 of 734 districts reach only 39.5% of the gap; you need about 300 districts to pass three-quarters. Compare Ghana, where 25 hospitals bring 86% of the carpet-viper burden within reach. India's burden is genuinely diffuse across a vast rural population, so a pilot in a handful of districts will not move the national figure. That points the strategy at the product side — getting region-appropriate antivenom into the national supply — with siting as the sequencing question, not the solution.
What's at stake — honest. This is a coverage-gap map, and we do not claim deaths averted. Burden is real (Million Death Study state death rates × 2011 census district population, rural-weighted; national 55,656 vs MDS ~58,000). The ASV coverage-gap is an evidence-informed zone approximation (Senji Laxme & Sunagar antivenomics 2019/2021; TRSTMH 2025) — it flags where the standard antivenom is likely to underperform, not a per-patient prediction. The output is a target list for region-specific antivenoms and stocking, not a placement of one product.
Correction carried prominently. An earlier draft of this work stated that no South-Asian antivenom products had been WHO risk-benefit-assessed. That was wrong: seven are (Bharat, Biological E ×2, Haffkine, Premium ×2, VINS). We correct it here rather than quietly drop it. The finding survives the correction, because it never depended on it: WHO assessment covers the Big-Four label, and does not certify performance against regional venom variation within those four species, nor against the ~20 medically important non-Big-Four species. That distinction is the whole point of this map.
Limits, by design. District population join matched 85.7% by name (unmatched districts are filled to their state's average so state totals stay exact). The hospital layer (NIC HealthGIS, hospital-tier: district/taluka hospitals + CHCs) covers 25 of 36 states — access is under-counted where facilities are missing, so the 0.6% "far from care" figure is a floor, not a ceiling. Coverage-gap fractions are state-level ordinal tiers, not district-specific measurements. A known tension we do not paper over: the tiers imply a "distance from the southern sourcing zone" gradient, but the underlying data does not have that shape — the antivenom met its marketed claim against the spectacled cobra in only one tested population (Andhra Pradesh), and performed as poorly in the Gangetic belt as in the northwest. The mainland tier survives only because Russell's viper and the saw-scaled viper, which dominate mortality there, are well covered. That is the model's reasoning, not a measurement. Rural weighting uses the census rural/urban household split as a proxy for the population split. Not clinical guidance.
